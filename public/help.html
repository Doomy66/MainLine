How to play MainLine

You rule an Empire, a chain of worlds a jump-1 ship can cross, and every other Empire in the sector is ruled by somebody else. Most of them are the computer. Build a fleet, take worlds and hold on to your own. One turn is a day and a jump takes a week.

Your first game

  1. New game. Driftwater Reach is a good first sector. Leave the area as the whole sector. The table lists every Empire, with its size, its capital's profile, its best tech level, what it earns a week and the credits it starts with. Click an Empire, in the table or on the map, to take it. A big, rich Empire is easier. One whose capital has a class A starport can build its own starships.
  2. Leave the rules as they are and press Start the game. Starting wealth multiplies every faction's opening treasury, up to five times, for a bigger war from the first day; it does not change what worlds pay each week. News travels starts at Instantly, which is the easiest way to learn the game; slower news makes the fog of war thicker.
  3. Buy your first fleet. For this one purchase the whole catalogue is on offer, whatever your worlds can build. Click a class's name to see its design sheet. A good start is two or three ships of jump-2 or better with real guns, such as a Broadsword or Patrol Corvette, plus something cheap to guard home. Keep a few MCr back for fuel and missiles. Press Done: sail.
  4. Find a target. Click nearby systems. The system pane shows who holds each world, what it is worth, and how strong its defences are. An independent world with weak defences, one or two jumps away, is a good first conquest.
  5. Set standing orders. On the Fleets tab, pick your fleet and choose the preset Seek and destroy. That fleet will now attack any enemy it meets and besiege any world it does not hold.
  6. Jump. Press Jump… and click the target on the map. Solid green rings are in range now. Dashed rings need stops on the way, and the fleet will refuel at each. Then press Wait for news, and the days run until your fleet arrives.
  7. Go in. Your fleet arrives at the jump point. Press Orbit to send it to the main world and end the day. Its guns will start on the defences. When they are silenced the world submits after a few more days, so long as your fleet stays.
  8. Keep going. Your treasury fills at the end of each week. Spend it at a shipyard, repair at worlds you hold, and look at the Reports tab for battles, sightings and captures. Click the name of a place in a report to go to it on the map.

The screen

The map is the sector as PlanetHex draws it. The wheel zooms, dragging moves it, and clicking picks a system. Worlds are dots, sized by population and coloured by what their surface is like: blue for water, tan for a dusty atmosphere, grey for airless rock, and a hollow ring where nobody lives. Hexes are washed in the colour of the faction that holds them, and a capital has a ring round it. Zoom in to see starports, hex numbers, naval bases (★) and gas giants.

Rest the pointer on a system to see its name, who holds it, its starport, population and tech level, and any other Empire's fleets there. Your fleets are little ships beside their worlds, heading right. Other Empires' fleets are ships heading up, ringed in red; a faint dashed one is where a fleet was last reported, not where it is now. The system pane says when each one arrived and when it was last seen, and lists fleets that have gone under Gone, with the days they were there. A fleet in jump space is a dot on a dashed line between two systems. A red dashed ring is a siege, and a burst marks a battle today.

TabWhat it is for

ShipyardLay down new ships at a yard you hold, or at an independent one. The catalogue is in four sections: warships; system defence, boats and fighters with no jump drive; capital ships of 5,000 tons and more, closed until you open it; and civilian and support ships (traders, liners, scouts), closed too. Import designs from the Ship Designer.
EmpireTreasury, income, upkeep, what it takes to win, standings, and your worlds.
ReportsEverything you have heard, newest first. Each report has an icon for its kind (battle, capture, arrival, sighting, new ship, money, loss) and every faction it names carries its emblem, a shape in its colour. Reports that do not concern your own fleets, worlds or treasury are dimmed. Battle reports open to show every round.

Save writes the whole game to one .game file. The game also keeps itself in the browser, so Carry on on the title screen picks up where you left off; if the browser ever refuses to keep it, the top bar says so, and Save is the way to keep the game.

The panel on the right can be made wider or narrower by dragging its left edge. Double-click the edge for the usual width.

A day

Give orders, then press End day. With several players, each takes a turn giving orders for the same day and hands the screen on. When the last is done, everybody's orders happen at once. The computer's factions give theirs at the same moment.

A fleet can do one of these in a day:

  • Move anywhere in its system: the main world's orbit, a gas giant, the planetoid belt or the jump point.
  • Jump to another system, or start a route of several jumps.
  • Refuel, going to wherever in the system it can.
  • Repair at a class A to D starport you hold.

Orders last until they are done, so a fleet on a route of four jumps needs telling once. Wait for news lets the days run, up to thirty, until something happens you should see: a battle, an arrival, a capture, a sighting, a ship commissioned.

Jumping and fuel

A jump takes a week. It burns a tenth of each ship's hull in fuel for every parsec, so a 400-ton ship needs 40 tons to jump one parsec and 80 to jump two. A fleet can jump as far as its shortest-legged ship, and no further than the fuel in every ship's tanks. Ships arrive at the system's jump point.

WhereFuelPrice
Class A or B starportRefinedCr500 a ton
Class C or D starportUnrefinedCr100 a ton
Gas giantUnrefined, for ships with fuel scoops or a streamlined hullFree
An ocean on the main worldUnrefined, for streamlined and partly streamlined shipsFree

Nobody sells fuel to an enemy; skimming a gas giant still works. Jumping on unrefined fuel risks a rough jump, which arrives a few days late. A ship with a fuel processor refines its own.

Order a jump beyond range and the fleet plans a route through systems where every ship in it can refuel, and stops to fill up at each.

An Empire starts as every world a jump-1 ship can reach, so a jump-1 ship can never leave its own Empire. To attack anyone you need jump-2 or better, and so does every ship in the fleet.

Carriers

A carrier comes with the fighters its design carries, as ships of their own in its fleet: the Hyperion with ten Snub fighters, the Azhanti High Lightning with sixty Ramparts, the Arakoine with a hundred Wasp heavy fighters. Their price is in the carrier's. Fighters in a carrier's hangars jump with it and need no tenders, and in battle they fight as the ships they are, and are lost as ships are. Replace them at any yard that builds small craft and merge them into the carrier's fleet; the fleet pane says how many of its hangars are filled. A fighter that fits no hangar needs a tender like any other boat, and holds back the whole fleet: buy fighters only to replace losses, since a new carrier's hangars are already full. The Shipyard tags each carrier with what it comes with, the fleet pane warns when a fleet has craft with no hangar, and a merge that would leave some without one asks first. To swap a carrier's fighters for others, split the old ones off into a fleet of their own and merge the new ones in.

Jump tenders

Ships with no jump drive, such as system defence boats, fighters and pinnaces, can still go to war if something carries them. A class A or B starport that will deal with you has jump tenders for hire. Tick Hire jump tenders on the fleet before choosing its jump. The tenders are rated jump-3, so the fleet jumps up to three parsecs, or less if another ship in it has a shorter drive. They charge Traveller's freight rates for every ton they carry, per jump: Cr1,000 a ton for one parsec, Cr1,600 for two, Cr2,600 for three. The fee is paid as each jump starts. The tenders stay with the fleet for the whole route, refuelling themselves, and leave when it arrives. Cancel the route part way and they leave there.

Standing orders

Your fleets meet the enemy while you are elsewhere, so each fleet carries standing orders that say what to do about it.

OrderWhat it does
EngageAttack any enemy fleet it meets, only fleets it clearly outguns, or nobody unless attacked first.
Target firstWhat its guns pick out: warships, the largest, the smallest, the most damaged, or unarmed ships.
BesiegeAttack the defences of any world it is orbiting and does not hold, and stay until it submits. A fleet on its way somewhere, or stopping to refuel on a route, leaves the world alone; it lays siege only when it has no orders or has been sent to the world's orbit.
InterceptGo after enemy fleets it would engage that are elsewhere in the same system.
EvadeTry to break off whenever attacked.
Withdraw atTry to break off once the fleet is down to this share of its hull.

Combat

Battle happens wherever hostile fleets are in the same place and one of them means to fight. At a main world, its defences join in. A day of battle is three rounds, and every weapon fires once a round.

  • Hitting. Roll two dice and add the gunner (+1), the ship's Fire Control software and its sensors. Take off the target's Evade software, which can be no more than its Thrust. Eight or more hits, and anything over eight is the Effect. Bays and spinal mounts are clumsy against small ships: −2 against anything up to 2,000 tons and −4 up to 100.
  • Damage. The weapon's dice plus the Effect, less the target's armour, times the mounting's multiple: 1 for a turret, 3 for a barbette, 10, 20 or 100 for a small, medium or large bay, and 1,000 for a spinal mount. Armour piercing weapons ignore some armour; meson guns ignore all of it.
  • Missiles and torpedoes come in salvos from a magazine that runs dry. They hit more easily, but point defence batteries and laser turrets shoot some down, and each one that gets through hits on its own. Reload at a class A to C starport you hold.
  • Defences. Sandcasters take a die off laser hits. Meson screens blunt meson guns; nuclear dampers blunt radiation weapons.
  • Critical hits. A ship takes one for every tenth of its hull it loses, and one for any hit with an Effect of 6 or more. They knock out weapons, drives, the jump drive, sensors, the computer and crew, or hole the fuel tanks. A ship with a wrecked jump drive cannot jump until repaired.
  • Breaking off. A fleet that wants out rolls 8+ on two dice, plus its Thrust less that of the fastest enemy chasing it. A fleet that gets away moves elsewhere in the system. Only a real threat can hold you: a chaser must be armed, armoured and able to move, in a force at least a quarter as strong as yours. A world's batteries never chase, and a few boats that cannot hurt you do not keep you under its guns.

Every battle report on the Reports tab opens to show each round: for every weapon of every ship at every target, how many fired, what they needed to hit and why, how many missiles were shot down, how many hit and for how much, and every critical. If a battle went strangely, the answer is in there.

Planetary defences

In orbit of its main world, every world with people on it and a tech level of 7 or better fights back. It has two kinds of defence, and to take the world you must beat both.

Ground batteries

Buried guns, missile silos and bunkers, counted together as one target. Gunners choosing what to shoot treat the batteries as no bigger than the largest ship defending the world, so they draw no more fire than that ship would; orbital-strike and bombardment weapons, which are poor against ships, go for them first. A world has one battery for every population digit over three, three more for a naval base and one for a scout base. Their hull points grow with population and tech level; a naval base adds 300. What they shoot with depends on tech level:

Tech levelBatteries fireAgainst ships
7 to 8Missiles, two a battery, 4D eachA nuisance
9 to 10Lasers, three a battery, 2D eachMostly stopped by armour
11Particle barbettes, 4D × 3Hurts
12 to 13Particle batteries, 6D × 3Hurts badly
14 and upMeson batteries, 5D × 10, straight through armourDeadly: the deep-site meson guns of Traveller's setting

Their fire control is +1 at TL10 and 11 and +2 from TL12, with a gunner's +1. Like any bay, the meson batteries are clumsy against small ships: −2 against anything up to 2,000 tons, −4 up to 100.

The planetary navy

High Guard defends worlds with ships, and so does Mainline. A world of TL9 or better and population 5 or more keeps system defence boats in orbit: one for every two population digits over three, two more at a naval base. They are the best armed boats without a jump drive that the world could build: Pinnaces up to TL12, the Dragon system defence boat from TL13. A world that can build none makes do with the nearest thing it can import. The boats fight beside the batteries as ships do, with their own armour, guns and missiles, and they can chase a fleet that tries to leave. The system pane shows how many a world has.

Garrisons

A fleet at a world you hold can be given to its defences with Add to defences: handy for stranded boats, or ships not worth the trouble of moving. The ships go to main world orbit and guard it for good, under the Guard standing orders, and join any garrison already there. They leave your fleets and the map, and take no more orders; the System tab and the map's hover card show the world's garrison. They still repair, refuel and cost their upkeep, fight anyone who comes into orbit, and must be beaten before a siege can take the world. If the world is lost anyway, its garrison is lost with it.

Their strengths

  • Armour. Batteries are dug into rock: their armour is four-fifths of the world's tech level, 9 at TL12 and 11 at TL14. A beam laser, 1D, cannot get through that at all, and a pulse laser, 2D, rarely does. A fleet of laser ships will scratch the paint.
  • Point defence. From TL10 a world shoots down missiles: two dice of them a round for every population digit over four. Each boat with a point defence battery stops more. A dozen missiles thrown at a big world may all be stopped.
  • Screens. From TL12 a nuclear damper blunts particle and fusion weapons; from TL13 a meson screen blunts mesons.
  • Depth. Batteries have deep magazines and the navy reloads between fights. Left alone, batteries rebuild a tenth of their strength a day, damaged boats mend a fifth a day, and one lost boat is replaced every week.

Their weaknesses

  • They cannot move. Batteries fight only in orbit of their own world, and never chase. A fleet at the jump point, the gas giant or the belt is out of their reach.
  • Small worlds are soft. A world of a few thousand people has no batteries to speak of, and below TL9 no navy. Take those first.
  • What gets through armour. Missiles in numbers (enough to swamp the point defence), barbettes, bays, armour-piercing weapons such as railguns, fusion and plasma guns, and above all meson guns, which ignore armour entirely.
  • They are not rebuilt under fire. Keep a fleet in orbit and the batteries stay down and the lost boats stay lost.

How to take one

  • Look before you jump: the system pane gives the world's population, tech level, defences and navy.
  • Bring the right guns. Check each class's weapons on its design sheet; a ship whose only weapons are beam lasers is for fighting other ships, not worlds.
  • Bring enough. At TL12 expect to need a squadron; at TL14 and up, capital ships or a great many missile boats.
  • Set a sensible withdrawal point on the fleet's standing orders, so a siege that is going wrong ends before the fleet does.

Taking a world

A world falls to a fleet that silences its defences and holds its orbit.

  1. Bring armed ships into orbit with Besiege on in their standing orders.
  2. Silence the batteries and destroy the planetary navy. That may take several days of battle.
  3. Hold the orbit, with none of the owner's armed ships there, until the world submits. That takes two days plus one for every two digits of population. The siege resets if you leave or are driven off.

A captured world's batteries start at a quarter strength, and it has no navy: the new owner's boats arrive one a week. Batteries rebuild a tenth a day once nobody is shooting at them.

Losing your capital

Lose your capital and you rule from your next biggest world, but the government falls with it. For 28 days the faction is in disorder: it collects only a quarter of its income and nothing on its slips moves. Worse, each of its other worlds may take the chance to break away and declare itself independent. The chance is one in ten, three in a hundred more for every parsec from the new capital, and one in ten more on a world whose law level is 3 or less, to no more than six in ten. Guard your capital.

Money and shipyards

At the end of every week each world pays its owner. It pays more for more people, higher tech and a better starport, and more again for a rich, industrial or agricultural world. A billion people at TL12 with a class A port pay about MCr35 a week. The same week, the upkeep of your ships comes out.

A yard can only have so many ships on the slips at once: three at a class A starport, two at class B and one at class C. Order more and they wait their turn, each starting when a slip comes free; the Shipyard tab shows how busy a yard is and when each ship will start and finish. An independent yard's slips are shared by everybody who builds there.

A ship's time on the slips starts from the book's day for every MCr1 it costs, scaled by the shipyard speed chosen for the game. Anything that would take more than a month is built in modules side by side, as High Guard allows for big ships, so the time grows much more slowly than the price. At the usual speed, on one slip, a fighter takes a week, a patrol corvette about five weeks, a 1,000-ton cruiser seven, a 5,000-ton carrier three months and an Azhanti High Lightning seven and a half.

A ship of 1,000 tons or more also takes any slip at her yard that nobody else needs, and each slip does a share of the work: alone at a class A yard, a big ship builds three times as fast. Every ship on order still gets a slip before a big one gets a second, so an order placed later takes a slip back and the big ship slows again. Her due day moves with this; the Shipyard tab shows it, and how many slips each ship has.

Repairs happen at worlds you hold, whenever a fleet is in orbit and not fighting. Independent class A yards build for anybody for 20% more. A new ship joins a Yard fleet in orbit when it is finished, with a report to say so; how long it takes depends on the shipyard setting chosen at the start.

News and the fog of war

News arrives instantly unless you choose otherwise, at setup or on the Empire tab. Set it to travel by ship and it takes its time. A report of anything happening away from your capital takes a week for every jump a courier would need to bring it: an enemy fleet arriving or leaving, a battle between other factions, a world changing hands. The map shows what your capital has heard. Other fleets appear where they were when last reported, and worlds show the owner the news last named.

Normally you see your own fleets and worlds as they are now, and news of anything they are part of reaches you at once: their battles, arrivals, sieges and captures. Your fleets do not wait for you either. They act on their standing orders the moment they meet the enemy.

Full fog of war

Turn on Full fog and your own fleets report by courier too. Each one appears where it was when its last report was sent, marked with how old that report is, and you hear of its battles when a courier gets home. Orders go the same way: an order to a fleet five weeks away lands five weeks later, and the fleet plans its route from wherever it has got to by then. Orders not yet delivered are listed on the fleet as in the post. A fleet lost in battle stays on your map until the news of it arrives. Splitting and merging fleets can only be done at your capital, where you can see the ships.

How fast news travels, and full fog, can both be changed at any time on the Empire tab, for every player at once. Turn full fog off and every order in the post arrives at once.

Winning

Hold the share of the peopled worlds in play chosen at the start, half unless you changed it, or be the last faction standing. A faction with no worlds and no ships is out. The Empire tab shows the standings and how many worlds you need.

Tips

  • Leave something at home. The computer will come for your capital, and it looks for weak spots.
  • Keep an eye on fuel. A fleet that runs dry at a world with no gas giant and a hostile starport is stuck.
  • Missile boats hit hard but run out. Send them home to reload, or mix them with lasers.
  • Big bays and spinal mounts are wasted on small ships; a swarm of fighters can tie them up.
  • Independent worlds near your Empire are cheap growth early on. Enemy capitals are the hardest nuts in the sector.
  • A fleet that keeps losing ships is telling you something. Raise its withdrawal point.
  • The computer sees no more than you would in its place. It knows nothing of a system where it has no ships and no worlds.

Your own sectors and ships

Sectors. Make one in PlanetHex. When you save a sector there, it writes a .sec file beside the .sector file. In Mainline's new game screen, press Load a PlanetHex .sec… and pick it. Sparse or Rift density makes the best game. At Standard density the worlds join up so well that one Empire can cover half the sector. Any other tool that writes the same tab-separated sector format works too.

Ships. Design one in the Traveller Ship Designer and save it as a .ship file. On the Shipyard tab, press Import a .ship design…. It joins the catalogue for this game and travels inside the saved game. A design has to be legal under the designer's rules. Everything Mainline knows about a ship comes off its design sheet: hull, armour, drives, fuel, weapons, software, sensors and price.

Suggestions

Found something wrong, or want something it does not do? Open an issue on GitHub and say what happened, or what you would like.

Designed or found a ship that earns its place? The catalogue grows from players' designs. Open an issue, attach the .ship file from the Traveller Ship Designer, and say where the design comes from: your own, or a published ship you have rebuilt. Designs that are legal under the designer's rules and fill a gap go in for everybody.
